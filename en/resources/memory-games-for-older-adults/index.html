Memory games for older adults: 5 ideas to enjoy together

Cards, music, words, and memories for an enjoyable time together. Choose what the person finds entertaining, without rushing or pressure to get answers right.

By the SELMI Team · Published September 27, 2026 · Updated October 15, 2026 · 5-minute read

Memory games for older adults can be a reason to share a conversation and an enjoyable activity. Use household objects, photos, or cards; there's no need to buy equipment or follow a demanding routine.

These five suggestions are recreational. Choose one, explain the rules with an example, and ask if they would like to try it. Adjust the difficulty as you play: working together and giving clues are welcome too.

1. Find matching pairs with cards or pictures

Find three pairs of easy-to-distinguish pictures, such as flowers, animals, or familiar places. Shuffle the six cards face down and turn over two each turn. When a pair appears, leave it face up.

If it feels difficult, start with the cards face up or use fewer cards. For a greater challenge, add another pair. Use large pictures and a well-lit table.

2. Complete a familiar song

Choose songs the person knows and enjoys. One person hums a section, and the other tries to remember what comes next, its name, or who sang it. Take turns or find the answer together.

Allow room for any conversation that comes up: a dance, a party, or someone linked to that song. If they don't remember the title, simply listening together can be the activity.

3. Name words in a category

Choose a familiar topic: foods, clubs, cities, or tools. Take turns naming a word in that category. Keep a visible list to remember what has already been said.

Avoid a timer if it creates pressure. Another version is to start with a letter or describe an object for the other person to guess. Agree on the rules together.

4. Create a story from photos

Find two or three photos the person wants to share. Ask what catches their eye and what they remember about that moment. If they prefer, they can make up a story from the pictures.

Listen without turning the conversation into a test of names or dates. The person decides what to tell and which photos to show. If a memory feels uncomfortable, change the subject.

5. Spot which object changed

Put three everyday objects on the table, such as a cup, a key, and a spoon. Look at them together. Then, while one person looks away, move or remove one object.

The other person tries to spot the change. Look again, give clues, or switch roles. Choose safe objects that are easy to handle.

How to adapt the activity to each person

  • Ask what they would like to play and respect it if they don't feel like it today.
  • Find a comfortable place with good lighting and little noise.
  • Use letters, pictures, and objects they can easily distinguish.
  • Try a short round and agree whether to continue or rest.
  • Focus on enjoying the moment together; don't compare scores with other people.

What a game can tell you about memory

Getting more or fewer answers right in a game cannot diagnose a memory problem. The US National Institute on Aging advises caution about claims that certain digital games improve memory or thinking. These suggestions are not a treatment or a clinical test.

If memory changes concern the person or their family, discuss them with a healthcare professional.
