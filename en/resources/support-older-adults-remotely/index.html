How to support an older adult from afar

Being far away does not mean being absent. Build a contact routine that nurtures the relationship, respects independence, and makes it clear how to ask for help.

By the SELMI Team · Published September 16, 2026 · Updated October 15, 2026 · 5-minute read
In this guide1. Agree on support2. Create a simple routine3. Have meaningful conversations4. Use reminders5. Prepare a planChoosing technology

Supporting an older adult from another home, city, or country often brings a mix of love, worry, and guilt. No tool replaces human presence, but clear arrangements can keep the relationship from revolving only around whether they took their medication or feel okay.

Independence should be the starting point. In Argentina, older adults have the right to make decisions about their care, privacy, and how they want to live. Providing support means offering help and options, rather than taking control without consent.

1. Agree together on what help is useful

Before installing an app or dividing family responsibilities, talk with the person. Ask what they want to handle independently, where they would welcome help, and what information they agree to share.

  • Decide who the main contacts will be.
  • Clarify which situations should trigger an alert.
  • Agree on times that respect their rest and activities.
  • Review the agreement as their needs change.
A good sign: the older adult understands the purpose of the support and can change their mind or ask for adjustments.

2. Create a simple, predictable routine

An overly complicated schedule is often abandoned. Start with a few actions that really matter: a call, medication prescribed by their healthcare professional, an appointment, or an activity they enjoy.

One possible example

  • Morning: a greeting and a brief look at the day ahead.
  • Midday: an agreed reminder, if needed.
  • Afternoon: an activity, walk, or conversation.
  • Once a week: review the upcoming schedule together.

3. Make contact about more than checking up

Meaningful social connections matter for wellbeing. Conversations can include memories, music, recipes, family news, or plans; they do not need to feel like an interrogation.

Try open questions: “What was the best part of your day?”, “Who would you like to talk to this week?”, or “What would you like to plan for tomorrow?”. Listening without rushing is also a way of offering support.

4. Use reminders as support

Reminders can help organize a routine, but they should not change medical instructions or replace professional follow-up. Record only confirmed information and agree on what happens if the person does not respond.

Important: do not change doses, schedules, or treatments on your own. If you have health questions, consult the appropriate professional.

5. Prepare a plan for important situations

The family should know whom to contact and what to do if a call goes unanswered, an important reminder is not confirmed, or the person asks for help. Write down phone numbers, trusted neighbors, and appropriate services for each situation.

A technology assistant is not an emergency system. In a real emergency, contact the local emergency service.

What to consider before choosing technology

  • Simplicity: natural speech, large text, and a few easy actions.
  • Consent: clear controls over what is remembered and shared.
  • Privacy: understandable information about data and deletion.
  • Personalization: routines and contacts chosen by the family and the person.
  • Clear limits: no promises of diagnoses or of replacing professional care.
