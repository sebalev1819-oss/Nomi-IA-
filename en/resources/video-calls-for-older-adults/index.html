Video calls for older adults: a family guide

Prepare a video call with the person: agree on a time, check clear sound, and choose controls they understand. The goal is to share a moment.

By the SELMI Team · Published October 5, 2026 · Updated October 6, 2026 · 3-minute read

1. Agree on when and whom they want to talk to

Ask which times suit them, whom they want to see, and how long they prefer to talk. A planned call may fit their day better than several unexpected calls.

If the family lives abroad, write both local times in the calendar. For a first trial, choose a quiet moment and just one caller. Add other relatives later if the person enjoys it.

Being able to call does not mean being available all the time. Also agree on how to let others know if they would rather rest or change the time that day.

2. Prepare the space and sound

Place the screen somewhere stable, at a height that lets the person look at it without holding it. Check that the camera frames them and that light falls in front of them, without a very bright window behind.

Test the sound in that room. If speech is hard to understand, turn down the TV or other noise and check the call volume. The W3C identifies contrast, easy-to-use controls, and understandable audio as relevant needs for many older adults.

  • Choose a comfortable chair and a stable table.
  • Check that the connection works where the device will be placed.
  • Keep cables out of walkways.
  • Test the camera and sound before a long conversation.

3. Practice the important steps

Try answering, ending a call, and returning to the home screen together. Explain one action at a time and let the person do it at their own pace. If something is difficult, adjust the device before adding more instructions.

If the system offers automatic answering, review who is authorized to use it and agree on how it is activated. Devices offer different options: ask about the available features and controls.

A useful trial: make a short call, hang up, and repeat it. Ask what was clear and what they would like to change.

4. Bring an idea to share

A video call can include a conversation about their interests, a family photo, a song, or an anecdote. You do not need to fill the time with questions about health or outstanding tasks.

If several people join, try speaking one at a time and leaving room for the older adult to participate. A short call they enjoy may be more pleasant than a long one where they only listen to others.

For variety, choose an activity from our guide to memory games to enjoy together. Ask first whether they feel like playing: the call can also be just a conversation.

5. Protect privacy and keep another way to make contact

Decide together which contacts can call and which options will be enabled. Respect their choice not to answer. If you want to save an image or record the conversation, ask permission first and explain how it will be used.

Check the frame to avoid showing documents, personal information, or spaces they do not want to share. A camera helps people talk; it should not become a way to watch without agreement.

A call can fail because of connectivity, a switched-off device, or because the person is busy. Keep a phone or another alternative way to make contact. An unanswered video call alone does not tell you what is happening.

Frequently asked questions about video calls

Do they need to know how to use a phone?

It depends on the chosen system. A dedicated device can simplify some steps, but try it with the person and confirm how to answer and end a call.

What should I do if they struggle to hear?

Try a quieter room, check the volume, and speak one person at a time. Ask which change feels more comfortable.

Can I use a video call to check how they are after an alert?

It can be a way to get in touch and talk. It cannot independently assess a medical situation or replace a visit, professional care, or emergency services.
